## Overview

### What is Flavobacteriosis (Flavobacterium spp.)?

Flavobacteriosis is an umbrella term for diseases caused by Gram-negative, non-flagellated bacteria of the genus *Flavobacterium* (family Flavobacteriaceae, phylum Bacteroidota), which are either non-motile or move by gliding rather than via flagella [1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jare.2014.10.009). Of the more than 100 described *Flavobacterium* species, three are recognized as the "big three" fish-pathogenic flavobacteria: *F. psychrophilum*, the agent of bacterial cold-water disease (BCWD, the term generally used in North America) and rainbow trout fry syndrome (RTFS, the term generally used in Europe); *F. columnare*, the agent of columnaris disease; and *F. branchiophilum*, the agent of bacterial gill disease (BGD) [1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jare.2014.10.009).

The three species occupy distinct ecological niches and affect different tissues. *F. psychrophilum* is a psychrophilic pathogen of cold-water salmonid hatcheries — principally rainbow trout (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), Atlantic salmon (*Salmo salar*), and coho salmon (*O. kisutch*) — causing skin/gill lesions and systemic disease with reported mortality of 30–90% in juvenile fish, and is reported from Europe, North America, Chile, Japan, Korea, and Australia [4](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.14119) [15](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13567-017-0448-z). *F. columnare* has a far broader host range across both wild and cultured warm-water freshwater fish — including carp, channel catfish, goldfish, eel, perch, salmonids, and tilapia — and is distributed worldwide; in the US channel catfish industry it is the second most prevalent disease-causing bacterium after *Edwardsiella ictaluri*, with yearly losses estimated at US$30 million [2](https://doi.org/10.1186/1297-9716-44-27). *F. branchiophilum* causes BGD with a tropism restricted to the gill epithelium, not normally invading deeper tissue, but severe infections can still produce morbidity and mortality of 10–75% [4](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.14119).

This chapter is scoped around *F. psychrophilum* and, more briefly, *F. branchiophilum*. *F. columnare* is referenced here only for context on the shared genus and overlapping management questions — it is the subject of its own dedicated future chapter.

## Clinical Signs of Flavobacteriosis (Flavobacterium spp.)

### Common Signs

- **Physical Signs:**
    - **F. psychrophilum — RTFS/BCWD:** Disease episodes typically occur at 10–14°C, with necrotic lesions on the skin surrounding the dorsal fin and tail. Affected fish show haemorrhage (mouth, dorsal fin, operculum, pelvic fin, trunk), splenomegaly with blurred spleen margins, trunk lesions, and an eroded tail and mouth [15](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13567-017-0448-z).
    - **F. columnare — Columnaris disease:** Pale necrotic gill lesions in young fish; in chronic cases, pale skin discoloration starting at the base of the dorsal fin that extends laterally to encircle the fish ("saddleback disease"), erosion progressing toward the caudal peduncle ("peduncle disease"), and ulceration of the oral mucosa ("mouthrot," or "cotton wool disease" in tropical fish) [2](https://doi.org/10.1186/1297-9716-44-27).
    - **F. branchiophilum — BGD:** Lesions are confined to the gill epithelium, which the bacterium colonizes without normally invading deeper tissue or internal organs [4](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.14119).

- **Behavioral Changes:**
    - **Reduced feeding:** Painful oral lesions in columnaris-affected fish render them anorectic, which can itself be a cause of death independent of the systemic infection [2](https://doi.org/10.1186/1297-9716-44-27).

**Progression of Clinical Signs**

- **Early Stages:** Disease onset is closely tied to water temperature. For RTFS, experimentally raising water temperature accelerated the appearance of clinical disease, while increasing salinity delayed onset and improved survival, suggesting a window in early infection where environmental management can buy time for diagnosis and treatment [9](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.70037).
- **Advanced Stages:** Untreated RTFS/BCWD and columnaris disease progress to the full lesion picture described above with high mortality; in columnaris disease, oral lesions can spread to the mandible and maxilla, and secondary fungal or bacterial infection of skin ulcers is common [2](https://doi.org/10.1186/1297-9716-44-27).
- **Impact on Fish Health:** Both live and dead fish shed *F. psychrophilum* into the water, but dead fish shed the bacterium at substantially higher rates and for longer durations than live fish, meaning that mortality events feed forward into further transmission pressure within a population if not managed promptly [10](https://doi.org/10.1128/spectrum.03601-23).

### Causes of Flavobacteriosis (Flavobacterium spp.)

**Etiology**

- **Causative Agents:** Members of the genus *Flavobacterium* are Gram-negative rods, 0.3–0.5 μm in diameter, that are non-flagellated and either non-motile or display gliding motility; the fish-pathogenic species are fastidious and generally do not grow on standard media such as trypticase soy agar [1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jare.2014.10.009). *F. psychrophilum* shows limited genomic diversity worldwide, with the MLST clonal complex CC-ST10 accounting for the majority of BCWD/RTFS outbreaks globally [5](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2018.00138).
- **Key Virulence Factors:** Colony spreading motility and proteolytic activity are required for pathogenicity in *F. psychrophilum* — in a comparative study, the only non-virulent isolate tested was deficient in both traits [6](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2019.01711). The *gldD* and *gldG* genes, part of the gliding-motility/Type IX secretion system (T9SS), are required for adhesion, biofilm formation, proteolytic activity, and full virulence; mutants deficient in either gene showed significantly reduced virulence in rainbow trout challenge models [7](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2017.02168). In *F. columnare*, virulence is morphotype-dependent: of three colony morphotypes studied, only the Rhizoid morphotype was virulent in rainbow trout, associated with a distinct internally organized cell structure, a secreted 13 kDa protein, and outer membrane vesicles [8](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2180-14-170).
- **Transmission Methods:**
    - **Horizontal Transmission:** Both live and dead fish shed *F. psychrophilum* into the surrounding water; dead fish shed the bacterium at markedly higher rates and for longer durations than live fish, and shedding dynamics vary by bacterial genetic variant and host species [10](https://doi.org/10.1128/spectrum.03601-23).
    - **Vertical Transmission:** *F. psychrophilum* has been isolated from milt, ovarian fluids, and in close association with eggs, supporting a vertical transmission route that operates alongside horizontal transmission [11](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms9050971).
    - **Vector/Environmental Transmission:** Standard iodine-based egg disinfection protocols in hatcheries are used to reduce pathogen load on eggs and limit transmission to the next generation; experimentally, bacteriophages have also shown short-term (24-hour) potential as a biocontrol agent against *F. psychrophilum* on eyed eggs without affecting egg survival [11](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms9050971).

**Risk Factors**

- **Environmental Factors:** *F. psychrophilum* and *F. columnare* occupy largely opposite thermal niches: RTFS onset accelerates with increasing water temperature [9](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.70037), whereas columnaris disease transmission and severity increase with rising temperature and organic matter load, with the gills serving as the major release site for the bacterium [2](https://doi.org/10.1186/1297-9716-44-27).
- **Farm Management Practices:** Prompt removal of dead and moribund fish is a key control point, since dead fish are disproportionate shedders of *F. psychrophilum* into hatchery and farm water [10](https://doi.org/10.1128/spectrum.03601-23).

### Diagnosis

**Diagnostic Methods**

- **Clinical Examination:** Gross lesions (saddleback/peduncle erosion for columnaris, haemorrhage and trunk lesions for RTFS, gill-restricted lesions for BGD) provide a presumptive diagnosis, but clinical signs overlap across the three species and with other bacterial diseases, so laboratory confirmation is required for species-level diagnosis.
- **Laboratory Tests:**
    - **Bacterial Culture:** The fish-pathogenic *Flavobacterium* species are fastidious and require specialized low-nutrient media (e.g., TYES or Shieh's medium); they do not grow on trypticase soy agar, and *F. branchiophilum* is considered the most fastidious of the three, which has limited the volume of research — including antimicrobial susceptibility studies — conducted on it [1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jare.2014.10.009) [4](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.14119).
    - **PCR-Based Methods:** A quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) assay targeting the *rpoC* gene allows highly sensitive and specific detection and quantification of *F. psychrophilum* directly from water and fish tissue samples without the need for culturing [12](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2180-14-105). A multiplex PCR assay enables simultaneous, accurate differentiation of *F. psychrophilum* from two other major salmonid pathogens, *Yersinia ruckeri* and *Aeromonas salmonicida* subsp. *salmonicida*, in a single reaction [13](https://doi.org/10.4142/jvs.2010.11.3.235).

**Differential Diagnosis**

- **Distinguishing Flavobacteriosis from Other Diseases:** Clinical signs of RTFS/BCWD overlap with enteric redmouth disease (*Yersinia ruckeri*) and furunculosis (*Aeromonas salmonicida*), making molecular confirmation necessary; the multiplex PCR assay validated for these three pathogens allows them to be ruled in or out from the same sample [13](https://doi.org/10.4142/jvs.2010.11.3.235).

### Treatment and Prevention

**Treatment Options**

- **Current Treatments:**
    - **Antibiotics:** Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) profiling of *F. psychrophilum* shows a consistent pattern across geographically distant farming regions: florfenicol retains high effectiveness, while resistance to oxytetracycline and quinolones is widespread. In Slovenia, 86.3% of isolates were non-wild-type (reduced susceptibility) for oxytetracycline and enrofloxacin and 90.2% for oxolinic acid, while all isolates remained fully susceptible to florfenicol and erythromycin; reduced oxolinic acid susceptibility was linked to a T83A or T83V substitution in the *gyrA* gene [4](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.14119). In Chile, MIC-based testing found non-wild-type frequencies of 70% for oxytetracycline and 38–45% for the quinolones oxolinic acid, flumequine, and enrofloxacin, but only 8% for florfenicol [14](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2016.01880).
    - **Treatment Challenges:** *F. branchiophilum* could not even be reliably classified as wild-type or non-wild-type for the antimicrobials tested in the Slovenian study, reflecting how its fastidious growth requirements have left its AMR profile far less characterized than that of *F. psychrophilum* [4](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.14119).

- **Vaccines:**
    - **Subunit and Attenuated Candidates:** No consistently effective, widely available commercial vaccine exists against *F. psychrophilum*, hampered by the bacterium's antigenic and genetic diversity. Candidate antigens — OmpH, OmpA, FspA, and lipopolysaccharide — have been identified using sera from convalescent rainbow trout, and an attenuated *exbD2* mutant strain achieved 81.8% relative percent survival (RPS) after intramuscular immunization and challenge [3](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12099).
    - **Polyvalent Immersion Vaccine:** A polyvalent immersion vaccine combining three formalin-inactivated *F. psychrophilum* strains gave 84% RPS in rainbow trout fry against a heterologous strain challenge, with vaccinated fish also showing a delayed mortality onset compared to unvaccinated controls [15](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13567-017-0448-z).
    - **Vaccine Implementation:** Commercial vaccines against *F. psychrophilum* remain available in only a limited number of countries (Chile, Norway), and none are suitable for the juvenile fry that bear the highest disease burden, leaving antibiotics as the primary recourse during an outbreak in most regions [15](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13567-017-0448-z).

**Preventive Measures**

- **Biosecurity Protocols:**
    - Iodine-based egg disinfection to reduce vertical/egg-associated transmission of *F. psychrophilum* [11](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms9050971).
    - Prompt removal of dead and moribund fish to reduce the disproportionate shedding load they contribute to hatchery and farm water [10](https://doi.org/10.1128/spectrum.03601-23).

- **Farm Management Practices:**
    - Managing water temperature and organic load where feasible, given the strong influence of both on transmission efficiency and severity of columnaris disease [2](https://doi.org/10.1186/1297-9716-44-27).

## Data Insights

### Disease Impact by Country

#### Slovenia

- **Flavobacteriosis Incidence:**
    - A survey of Slovenian farmed rainbow trout confirmed the presence of both *F. psychrophilum* (51 isolates) and *F. branchiophilum* (8 isolates), the first study to characterize AMR in *F. branchiophilum* using the broth microdilution method [4](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.14119).

- **Treatment & Management:**
    - 86.3% of *F. psychrophilum* isolates showed reduced susceptibility to oxytetracycline and enrofloxacin, and 90.2% to oxolinic acid, while all isolates remained fully susceptible to florfenicol and erythromycin. *F. branchiophilum* isolates could not be reliably classified as wild-type or non-wild-type with current methods, reflecting a broader diagnostic and surveillance gap for this species [4](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.14119).

#### Chile

- **Flavobacteriosis Incidence:**
    - *F. psychrophilum* is described as the most important bacterial pathogen of freshwater farmed salmonids in Chile, with 125 isolates characterized from reared salmonids showing clinical signs of flavobacteriosis [14](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2016.01880).

- **Treatment & Management:**
    - MIC-based epidemiological cut-off analysis found non-wild-type frequencies of 70% for oxytetracycline and 38–45% for the quinolones oxolinic acid, flumequine, and enrofloxacin, while only 8% of isolates showed reduced susceptibility to florfenicol — a resistance pattern broadly consistent with the Slovenian data above despite the geographic distance between the two surveys [14](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2016.01880).

## Conclusion

Flavobacteriosis represents not one disease but a complex of related diseases caused by distinct *Flavobacterium* species occupying different thermal niches and host tissues, united mainly by shared genus-level biology — fastidious growth requirements, gliding motility, and a reliance on similar virulence mechanisms (spreading motility, proteolytic activity, Type IX secretion). *F. psychrophilum* alone causes substantial, recurring losses in cold-water salmonid hatcheries worldwide, and *F. branchiophilum* remains a persistent if less-studied threat to gill health in the same systems.

The most effective management strategies currently available combine close water-temperature and organic-load management, prompt removal of dead and moribund fish to limit shedding, iodine-based egg disinfection to interrupt vertical transmission, and judicious antibiotic use guided by local resistance surveillance — florfenicol remains broadly effective against *F. psychrophilum* across geographically distant farming regions (Slovenia, Chile), even where resistance to oxytetracycline and quinolones is already widespread. No commercially robust, broadly available vaccine yet exists for *F. psychrophilum*, and none of the limited vaccines that do exist are suitable for the juvenile fry that bear the greatest disease burden, leaving antibiotics as the default response to most outbreaks.

The clearest knowledge gap identified during this research pass is *F. branchiophilum* itself: it is the most fastidious of the three major fish-pathogenic flavobacteria, and this has limited even basic antimicrobial susceptibility characterization — a 2025 Slovenian study could not reliably classify its isolates as wild-type or non-wild-type with current testing methods. Bacteriophage-based biocontrol of *F. psychrophilum*, while still preliminary, also merits continued investigation as a non-antibiotic intervention, particularly around the egg stage where vertical transmission can be intercepted. Country-level surveillance data for either pathogen remains sparse outside of the handful of farming regions covered here, and discrete, well-documented outbreak case studies — as opposed to general AMR surveys — were not identified for this chapter and remain an open item for a future research pass.
