## Overview

### What is Caligidosis (*Caligus rogercresseyi*)?

Caligidosis is the infestation of fish by *Caligus rogercresseyi*, a copepod of the family Caligidae commonly known as sea louse, which is endemic to wild fish in the marine and brackish waters of Chile. First described in 2000 by Boxshall and Bravo, it is the main sea louse species affecting salmon aquaculture in Chile. It feeds on the mucus, skin and blood of its host, altering the osmotic barrier, reducing appetite and feed conversion, and damaging the carcass, which causes substantial economic losses. In Chile, treatment against the parasite has been estimated to add USD 1.4 to the cost of producing each kilogram of salmon.[1](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/chi112982.pdf)[2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-92425-3)[3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2020.615039)

It has been described in all farmed salmonid species and in native wild fish such as róbalo, pejerrey and lenguado. Atlantic salmon (*Salmo salar*) and rainbow trout (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) are the most susceptible, while coho salmon (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*) has a lower susceptibility. Infestations were first reported in rainbow trout farmed in Los Lagos in the 1980s, then confirmed in Atlantic salmon in Aysén in the 1990s, and in 2017 the parasite was reported for the first time in Magallanes.[1](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/chi112982.pdf)[2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-92425-3)[3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2020.615039)

Its life cycle has eight stages: two nauplii and one copepodid, which are planktonic (the copepodid is the infective stage), four chalimus stages attached to the host, and the adult. Development is directly related to water temperature and salinity, and temperatures above 4.2 °C and salinities above 24 ppt accelerate its development and favour its proliferation.[1](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/chi112982.pdf)[2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-92425-3)[4](http://www.sernapesca.cl/sites/default/files/res.ex_.60-2022.pdf)

## Clinical Signs of Caligidosis (*Caligus rogercresseyi*)

### Common Signs

**Physical Signs**

The parasite feeds on skin, mucus and blood, so at its attachment and feeding sites it can break the continuity of the skin and cause scale loss, petechiae, ecchymoses, lacerations and, occasionally, eye lesions.[4](http://www.sernapesca.cl/sites/default/files/res.ex_.60-2022.pdf) In Atlantic salmon, fin condition worsened as parasite abundance increased in an experimental infestation, mainly in the pectoral, pelvic and anal fins.[5](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani10071166)

**Physiological and Behavioural Signs**

Infestation alters the osmotic barrier of the skin and reduces appetite and feed conversion.[3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2020.615039) In experimental infestation, fin damage was associated with higher plasma glucose and cortisol, which are indicators of stress, and previous work reported these changes with more than six adult parasites per fish. In that study, the authors could not separate the damage caused directly by the parasite from the effect of stress.[5](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani10071166)

**Secondary Infections**

Skin damage favours the entry of pathogens and increases susceptibility to bacterial and viral infections.[2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-92425-3)[4](http://www.sernapesca.cl/sites/default/files/res.ex_.60-2022.pdf) In a coinfection experiment, Atlantic salmon exposed to *Piscirickettsia salmonis* and to *C. rogercresseyi* copepodids reached 100% mortality, compared with 46% (251 of 545 fish) with *P. salmonis* alone.[6](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0095397)

### Causes of Caligidosis (*Caligus rogercresseyi*)

**Life Cycle**

*C. rogercresseyi* has eight stages: two planktonic nauplii, a planktonic copepodid that is the infective stage, four chalimus stages attached to the host by a frontal filament, and the adult. Adults can be males, females without egg sacs and ovigerous females, whose egg sacs release the eggs that hatch as nauplii I and restart the cycle.[4](http://www.sernapesca.cl/sites/default/files/res.ex_.60-2022.pdf) Development depends on water temperature: the cycle takes about 45 days at 10 °C, 32 days at 12 °C and 26 days at 15 °C. Planktonic stages measure between 425 and 658 µm, and chalimus stages 830 µm or more.[7](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2024/01/INFORME-TECNICO-PSEVC-CALIGIDOSIS-ENERO-2022.pdf)

**Transmission and Sources of the Parasite**

The parasite occurs naturally in wild fish such as róbalo, lenguado and pejerrey, free in the plankton, and on farmed salmonids.[4](http://www.sernapesca.cl/sites/default/files/res.ex_.60-2022.pdf) The larvae disperse through the water column and can reach farms up to 30 km from the farm of origin. The movement of live fish by vessel to holding facilities, where they can stay up to 7 days, is an important risk factor for its spread.[7](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2024/01/INFORME-TECNICO-PSEVC-CALIGIDOSIS-ENERO-2022.pdf) The microbiota of lice from Chilean farms includes potential fish pathogens such as *Vibrio*, *Tenacibaculum* and *Aeromonas*, which suggests that lice could act as reservoirs.[8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-59747-0)

**Risk Factors**

High stocking densities favour the proliferation and pathogenicity of the parasite.[4](http://www.sernapesca.cl/sites/default/files/res.ex_.60-2022.pdf) Water temperature, salinity and current velocity are the main environmental factors limiting its distribution in southern Chile, and habitat suitability models project new suitable areas for 2050 and 2100 under climate change scenarios.[3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2020.615039) Sernapesca's first surveillance programme (2007) identified farm density and the treatments carried out as risk factors associated with parasite loads.[7](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2024/01/INFORME-TECNICO-PSEVC-CALIGIDOSIS-ENERO-2022.pdf)

### Diagnosis

**Parasite Counts**

Diagnosis on farms is based on visual inspection and counting of the parasites on sampled fish. Lice are counted by stage in three categories: juveniles (chalimus I to IV), mobile adults (males and females without egg sacs) and ovigerous females, which are about 5 mm long and carry egg strings visible to the naked eye. Under the surveillance programme, a qualified sampler carries out these counts, and the average number of ovigerous females per fish is the indicator used to classify farms (see Regulatory Framework).[3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2020.615039)[4](http://www.sernapesca.cl/sites/default/files/res.ex_.60-2022.pdf)

**Treatment Efficacy**

The efficacy of an immersion treatment is measured by comparing the parasite loads in the same 4 cages the day before the treatment and 24 to 48 hours after it. A low efficacy can reflect a loss of sensitivity of the parasite, but Sernapesca notes that application problems must be ruled out first, such as product dilutions that do not follow the technical sheet or tarpaulin dimensions that do not match those used to calculate the dose.[7](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2024/01/INFORME-TECNICO-PSEVC-CALIGIDOSIS-ENERO-2022.pdf)

**Resistance Testing**

The sensitivity of a lice population is assessed with bioassays, in which lice collected from farms are exposed to a range of concentrations of the product. Bioassays on lice from Los Lagos showed increasing resistance to deltamethrin in 2008 and 2012 compared with 2007, before pyrethroids were introduced in Chile, and reduced sensitivity to azamethiphos was detected in 2014, a year after its introduction. A variant of the acetylcholinesterase gene *AChE1a* (F318V) was associated with lice surviving high azamethiphos concentrations, and is a candidate marker for resistance monitoring.[14](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12223)[15](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13071-018-3151-7)

Since 2017, the Instituto de Fomento Pesquero (IFOP) has run a programme to monitor the resistance of *C. rogercresseyi* to antiparasitics, which standardised a simplified bioassay, and IFOP leads the official bioassay surveillance defined by Sernapesca. In the ninth stage of the programme, presented in 2026, 365 valid bioassays from 117 samplings in 32 farm groupings showed a median of 63.7% of lice affected by azamethiphos at the therapeutic concentration, a level that has fallen over the stages but shows signs of stabilising. The median was close to 85% for deltamethrin and 93% for hydrogen peroxide, which has been stable since 2024, while cypermethrin has shown a stable response since the programme began, with susceptible populations predominating. The non-pharmacological product Lyptus Plus exceeded 93%.[7](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2024/01/INFORME-TECNICO-PSEVC-CALIGIDOSIS-ENERO-2022.pdf)[16](https://www.salmonexpert.cl/antiparasitarios-caligus-caligus-rogercresseyi/vigilancia-confirma-cambios-en-la-susceptibilidad-de-caligus-a-antiparasitarios/2145716)

### Treatment and Prevention

**Pharmacological and Non-Pharmacological Treatments**

Chile introduced regulations for the integrated pest management of sea lice in 2007, but pharmacological treatments remain the main control tool, and non-pharmacological treatments have been available since 2016. In 2021, 7.72 g of medicines were prescribed per tonne of harvested salmon, mainly azamethiphos (47%) and hexaflumuron (45.5%), together with 5,532 t of hydrogen peroxide.[17](https://doi.org/10.1111/raq.12815) Current use and the rules on treatment windows, rotation and usage caps are described under Data Insights.

**Declining Efficacy**

Treatment efficacy reported by producers fell significantly between 2016 and 2021 for the three most used active ingredients. The median efficacy of azamethiphos dropped from 99.4% in 2016 to 70.6% in 2021, and that of deltamethrin from 76.1% in 2016 to 62.3% in 2020. Hydrogen peroxide also declined but kept the highest efficacy of the period, with medians above 88.5%. Sernapesca relates this decline to the increasing resistance of the parasite reported in the literature.[7](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2024/01/INFORME-TECNICO-PSEVC-CALIGIDOSIS-ENERO-2022.pdf)

**Coordinated Treatments**

Immersion baths have no lasting effect, so lice from neighbouring farms can reinfest treated fish. In Chile, treating neighbouring farms (within 10 km by sea) in synchrony was associated with lower adult lice levels from weeks 5 to 7 after treatment. The official treatment windows coordinate immersion treatments within each farm grouping.[4](http://www.sernapesca.cl/sites/default/files/res.ex_.60-2022.pdf)[18](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.prevetmed.2016.11.011)

**Genetic Resistance and Vaccines**

Resistance to *C. rogercresseyi* in Atlantic salmon is moderately heritable. In post-smolts from a Chilean breeding programme, the heritability of lice density was 0.28, and three genomic regions explained 7 to 13% of the genetic variation in resistance, which supports selective breeding. Vaccines are being tested experimentally: recombinant cathepsin, alone or mixed with peritrophin, reduced the early-stage (chalimus) burden by 44 to 52% in Atlantic salmon, and a peptide vaccine designed from a *Lepeophtheirus salmonis* protein reduced adult *C. rogercresseyi* by 92% in a challenge trial in Chile.[19](https://doi.org/10.3389/fgene.2019.00056)[20](https://doi.org/10.3390/vaccines10071063)[21](https://doi.org/10.3390/vaccines12050456)

### Case Studies

**Establishment of *C. rogercresseyi* in Magallanes (2017 to 2026)**

*C. rogercresseyi* had been reported along northern Patagonia, between 40°15'S and 49°16'S, where about 90% of Chilean salmon farms are located. In 2017 it was found for the first time on farmed Atlantic salmon in the Magallanes region, south of 49°16'S. Eight farms had at least one louse that year, and three behaved as epidemic farms, with peaks of 24.5 adult and 14 juvenile lice per fish. Azamethiphos baths sharply reduced the lice on the two farms with the highest levels, but the fish were reinfested a few weeks later.[24](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aquaculture.2019.734386)

Surveillance data suggest that the parasite has since persisted and spread. In 2024, 8 farm groupings (ACS) in Magallanes reported lice, with a regional maximum of 0.10 ovigerous females per fish, and in 2025 the number rose to 10 ACS and the maximum to 0.30, although the weekly prevalence of farms with 3 or more ovigerous females fell from 5.56% to 2.6%. Antiparasitic baths began in the region in 2024, and Sernapesca set official coordination windows for immersion treatments in Magallanes for 2026.[25](https://www.radiopolar.com/evento-esporadico-riesgo-presente-caligus-comienza-instalarse-magallanes-quedarse)

In 2026, IFOP ran its susceptibility bioassays in Magallanes for the first time, at two farms. The assays were valid at Isla Capitán Aracena but could not be validated at Sector Taraba, where the parasites were quickly affected after removal from the fish, possibly because of freshwater influence or low water temperature. IFOP also observed phenotypic differences from lice in Los Lagos and Aysén (larger specimens and fewer skin petechiae) and suggests that the populations may differ genetically and respond differently to antiparasitics. It plans to expand the monitoring and to study the population genetics to define management units.[26](https://www.salmonexpert.cl/bioensayos-caligidosis-caligus/primeros-bioensayos-de-caligus-en-magallanes-revelan-nuevos-desafios-sanitarios-del-salmon/2114987)

**Lessons Learned:** The parasite spread south, from a first report to repeated detections over several years, while loads stayed well below those of Los Lagos and Aysén. Experts recommend acting while pressure is still low, through early surveillance, biosecurity and coordination among farms, and resistance data from other regions cannot be assumed to apply to the Magallanes population.

## Data Insights

### Disease Impact by Country

#### Chile

**Surveillance Programme**

Caligidosis is monitored through the Specific Health Programme for the Surveillance and Control of Caligidosis (PSEVC Caligidosis, Resolución Exenta N°60/2022) run by the National Fisheries and Aquaculture Service (Sernapesca). It covers high-surveillance centres (CAV) farming Atlantic salmon and rainbow trout in the Los Lagos, Aysén and Magallanes regions. The programme sets a threshold of 3.0 average ovigerous females (OF) per fish, and a CAV is classified as a high dissemination centre (CAD) when its first monitoring after the official treatment window of its farm grouping shows loads at or above that threshold.[9](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-Situacion-Sanitaria-Salmonicultura-Ano-2025.pdf)

**Parasite Loads in 2025**

In Los Lagos, the highest weekly average load was 3.0 OF (weeks 01 and 18) and the lowest was 1.6 OF (weeks 45 and 46). In Aysén, the highest was 3.6 OF (week 42, October) and the lowest was 1.7 OF. Magallanes had considerably lower loads, with a maximum weekly average of 0.3 OF. Compared with 2024, loads showed a slight downward trend in Los Lagos and a slight upward trend in Aysén, while water temperature remained stable in the three regions.[9](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-Situacion-Sanitaria-Salmonicultura-Ano-2025.pdf)

**Centres Above the Threshold**

In Los Lagos and Aysén, the weekly proportion of CAV with an average load of 3.0 OF or more ranged from 8.4% (week 49, December) to 25.4% (week 01, January), while in Magallanes the maximum was 2.6% (week 39, September). Over 2025, 211 CAV recorded loads of 3.0 OF or more, mostly in the heaviest production stage (T3), which accounted for 43.6% (92 centres). Sernapesca defines the production stages by fish weight: T1 is up to 1.5 kg for Atlantic salmon (up to 1.0 kg for rainbow trout and coho salmon), T2 is above 1.5 and up to 3.0 kg (above 1.0 and up to 2.0 kg), and T3 is above 3.0 kg (above 2.0 kg).[9](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-Situacion-Sanitaria-Salmonicultura-Ano-2025.pdf)

**Antiparasitic Use in 2025**

Sernapesca reports antiparasitic use for production cycles closed in 2025, meaning those whose harvest finished between 1 January and 31 December. The sample comprises 315 cycles: 195 of Atlantic salmon, 107 of coho salmon and 13 of rainbow trout. These products are used mainly to control *Caligus rogercresseyi*, together with non-pharmacological immersion strategies, whose use increased by 36.1% compared with 2024 (from 495 to 674 uses). Hydrogen peroxide is reported separately from the other antiparasitics because of its much larger volumes and its lower environmental impact, as it breaks down quickly into water and oxygen.[10](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-de-uso-de-antimicrobianos-ano-2025.pdf)

Antiparasitics other than hydrogen peroxide (azamethiphos, emamectin benzoate, deltamethrin and hexaflumuron) totalled 3.03 t, giving a national consumption index of 2.52 g per tonne produced, 41.26% lower than in 2024. Azamethiphos and hexaflumuron accounted for 91.2% of this use, with emamectin benzoate and deltamethrin used to a lesser extent. All are given as immersion baths except emamectin benzoate, which is delivered in the feed. Use was concentrated in Atlantic salmon and, to a lesser extent, rainbow trout, and no use was recorded in coho salmon, which Sernapesca relates to its lower susceptibility to caligidosis.[10](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-de-uso-de-antimicrobianos-ano-2025.pdf)

Hydrogen peroxide, which Sernapesca describes as the product applied to control caligidosis, was used as an immersion bath, mainly in Atlantic salmon in Los Lagos and Aysén. A total of 12,947 t were applied, giving an index of 10,795 g per tonne produced, which did not change significantly compared with 2024 (0.87%). Sernapesca considers it an effective alternative with a lower environmental impact for the control of caligidosis.[10](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-de-uso-de-antimicrobianos-ano-2025.pdf)

**Regulatory Framework**

Caligidosis is a List 2 high-risk disease in Chile.[11](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/09/RES-EX-N%C2%B0-2385-30-07-26-Modifica-PSEVC-caligidosis.pdf) It is regulated by the Specific Health Programme for the Surveillance and Control of Caligidosis, currently Resolución Exenta N°60/2022, which replaced the 2015 programme and was modified in 2026 to allow in-situ net cleaning and the return of the liquid fraction of uneaten feed capture systems.[4](http://www.sernapesca.cl/sites/default/files/res.ex_.60-2022.pdf)[11](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/09/RES-EX-N%C2%B0-2385-30-07-26-Modifica-PSEVC-caligidosis.pdf) The treatment window is the official period, set by Sernapesca for each farm grouping by region and season, within which immersion antiparasitic treatments must be applied. The table below summarises what producers must do in each sanitary scenario, according to the programme and Sernapesca's usage limits.[4](http://www.sernapesca.cl/sites/default/files/res.ex_.60-2022.pdf)[12](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2024/01/Limites-maximos-de-uso-de-antiparasitarios.pdf)

| Scenario | What the producer must do |
|---|---|
| Routine monitoring, every farm | Have a qualified sampler. High-surveillance farms (Atlantic salmon and rainbow trout in Los Lagos, Aysén and Magallanes) count parasites weekly in 6 consecutive cages (4 in Magallanes), sampling 10 fish per cage, and report the results by the first working day of the following week. Low-surveillance farms, such as coho salmon farms, sample 4 cages monthly. |
| Antiparasitic treatment | Obtain a veterinary prescription through the online platform (PMV online), treat by immersion in closed systems (tarpaulin or well boat) within the official treatment window of the farm grouping, for no more than 7 days, and notify Sernapesca at least 3 days before the window. Hydrogen peroxide and non-pharmacological treatments can be applied outside the window. A post-treatment efficacy report is due within 2 working days. |
| Choice of active ingredient | The same chemical family can be used at most 3 consecutive times per production cycle, and rotation requires another family, a non-pharmacological option or hydrogen peroxide. Pyrethroids and organophosphates by immersion are capped per farm and cycle, for example at 43 kg of active ingredient for the highest-use groupings of Los Lagos and Aysén and 23 kg in Magallanes (in force from February 2024 for those groupings and from February 2025 for the rest). |
| First monitoring after the treatment window at 3.0 OF or more (high dissemination centre) | Apply control measures in the next treatment window. A farm classified as a high dissemination centre 3 consecutive times must harvest 50% of its biomass within 21 days, and one classified 3 times within 6 consecutive evaluations must harvest 25% within 21 days, sending the harvest plan within 48 hours of notification. |
| Ways to avoid that classification | Sernapesca can exempt farms affected by adverse environmental conditions or exceptional health situations. A non-pharmacological treatment covering at least 50% of the populated cages can exclude the farm at the next evaluation, and a voluntary harvest plan (at least 15% of the biomass within 21 days, once per cycle) suspends the classification while it is carried out. |
| Harvest from a cage at 9.0 OF or more | Fish cannot stay in a floating holding facility. Harvest must go directly to the plant, to a land-based holding facility, or with the holding time reduced to 72 hours, otherwise Sernapesca can suspend the sanitary movement certificates. |

In practice, loads of 3.0 OF or more after a treatment window can end in the early harvest of a quarter to half of a farm's biomass.

**Antiparasitic Use and Efficacy in 2018**

The technical report cited in Resolución Exenta N°594 states that five active ingredients were registered against *C. rogercresseyi*: azamethiphos, cypermethrin and deltamethrin, used as immersion baths in sea farms, and lufenuron and emamectin benzoate, used in freshwater centres or at stocking in the sea. In 2018, 1,396 antiparasitic immersion treatments were reported, 77% of them with azamethiphos, while hydrogen peroxide, then used under a special use authorisation, accounted for less than 0.05% of treatments. Efficacy was highest for azamethiphos, followed by deltamethrin, but the report notes a decline in average efficacy, which had fluctuated between 90 and 100% in 2016-2017, with some areas reporting 80% or less.[13](https://www.subpesca.cl/portal/617/articles-103570_recurso_2.pdf)

## Research and References

### Latest Research Findings

**Dispersal Modelling**

IFOP's open-access CHONOS platform includes an operational oceanographic model for southern Chile (MOSA), which forecasts currents, temperature and salinity 3 and 10 days ahead, and a particle-tracking tool (PARTIMOSA) with a module calibrated to the biophysical behaviour of *Caligus*, including active swimming and environmental mortality, to estimate the risk of dispersal between neighbouring farms. Sernapesca uses these models to define pathogen containment measures, and they support the redesign of farm groupings (ACS).[22](https://infosalmon.cl/ifop-modelacion-oceanografica-salmonicultura-acs/)

The CLIC module of CHONOS shows connectivity matrices between salmon farm concessions for the planktonic stages of *C. rogercresseyi* (nauplius and the infective copepodid). Its individual-based model includes maturation between larval stages, mortality, and vertical migration between the surface and the halocline following day and night cycles, avoiding low-salinity waters associated with high mortality.[23](http://chonos.ifop.cl/wiki/clic-conectividad-lagrangiana/)
