## Overview

### What are Harmful Algal Blooms (HABs)?

- HABs (in Spanish, *floraciones algales nocivas*, FAN) are proliferations of microalgae that harm fish through toxins, gill damage or oxygen depletion. They are a non-infectious, environmental cause of mortality.
- They are sporadic but can kill whole sites within days. Fish-killing blooms are considered the greatest danger to the Chilean salmon industry [3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10458688/).
- Global losses to fish-killing HABs have been estimated at about US$8 billion per year [19](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/71988/noaa_71988_DS1.pdf).
- Main fish-killing groups affecting salmonid farming:
    - Raphidophytes: *Heterosigma akashiwo* [5](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9504362/)
    - Dictyochophytes: *Pseudochattonella* spp. (including *P. verruculosa*), *Octactis speculum* [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-19461-4) [14](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10467100/)
    - Dinoflagellates: *Karenia* spp. (including *K. mikimotoi*) [6](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10892511/) [17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11768604/)
    - Haptophytes: *Chrysochromulina leadbeateri*, *Prymnesium parvum*, *Phaeocystis pouchetii* [9](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1568988322001159) [12](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10302719/) [11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13331527/)

## Clinical Signs

### Common Signs

- **In the water and cages:**
    - Sudden mortality, often within hours to days of the bloom reaching the site, with floating carcasses [15](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10299462/).
- **At necropsy:**
    - Gills are the most affected organ, with tissue damage and circulatory disorders (Chile 2016) [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-19461-4).
    - Skin lesions, mucus on the gill surface and signs of asphyxiation (*H. akashiwo*) [15](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10299462/).
    - Gill irritation (*O. speculum*, Galicia) [14](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10467100/).
- **Mechanism of death:**
    - Not fully understood. For *P. verruculosa* in Chile 2016 the mechanism is unknown [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-19461-4).
    - *H. akashiwo*: likely high levels of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) at dense cell concentrations, combined with reactive oxygen species (ROS) [5](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9504362/). Another study found ROS were not the main cause of ichthyotoxicity [16](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6072012/).
    - In New Zealand, hypoxia was considered the primary cause in a *P. verruculosa* event, with inconclusive pathology [13](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9147682/).
- **Secondary infection:**
    - *Tenacibaculum maritimum* was isolated from Chilean Atlantic salmon mortalities associated with a *Pseudochattonella* bloom [8](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/08997659.2017.1339643).

### Causes

- **Non-infectious.** The cause is exposure to ichthyotoxic microalgae at sufficient cell density.
- **Climate and oceanography:**
    - Chile 2016: strong El Niño, higher surface temperature and reduced freshwater input weakened fjord stratification and brought saline, nutrient-rich water [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-19461-4).
    - Chile 2021: second driest year in seven decades, with less freshwater inflow into the fjords [4](https://i-mar.cl/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/2023_Diaz_etal_STOTEN.pdf).
    - Heat waves and drought are linked to massive algal proliferation in Patagonian fjords [3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10458688/).
    - *C. leadbeateri* grew best at salinity 28 to 30 and 13 to 15 °C [22](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1568988324001422).
    - *H. akashiwo* toxicity increased with warm, lower-salinity water [15](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10299462/).
- **Nutrients:**
    - Nutrient depletion was linked to mixotrophy and higher toxin production in *C. leadbeateri* [10](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12190008/).
- **Role of farming (open question):**
    - A Chilean study suggests organic nitrogen excreted by farmed fish could favour mixotrophic species such as *Karenia*. This is one of several hypotheses listed, not an established finding [6](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10892511/).
    - In Scotland, farmed salmon biomass had no significant effect on cell abundance of *Karenia mikimotoi*, *Alexandrium*, *Dinophysis* and *Pseudo-nitzschia* [18](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1568988323001385?via=ihub).
- **Perceived global increase:** partly explained by intensified monitoring and emerging bloom impacts [23](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-021-00178-8).

### Diagnosis

- **Monitoring and cell counts:**
    - Daily water sampling and phytoplankton counts are the usual early warning on farms [20](https://www.globalseafood.org/advocate/ca-technology-protect-salmon-farms-from-harmful-algal-blooms/).
    - Chile has dedicated HAB monitoring protocols [25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7589761/).
- **Remote sensing:** Sentinel-2 and Sentinel-3 satellite data were used to monitor blooms in southern Chile [7](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7544481/) [26](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12654718/).
- **Histopathology:** gill examination (Chile 2016) [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-19461-4).
- **Differential diagnosis:**
    - Jellyfish and hydrozoan blooms also cause gill damage and mortality in Norwegian farms [27](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12368256/).
    - Secondary bacterial disease such as tenacibaculosis can accompany a bloom [8](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/08997659.2017.1339643).
- **Causality caveat:** algal cells accumulate in gill mucus and lesions after the fact, so their presence alone does not prove cause [15](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10299462/).

### Treatment and Prevention

- **No treatment** for affected fish. Management is operational.
- **Operational responses:** feed withdrawal, minimising handling and stress, early harvest, relocating fish to another site [21](https://doi.org/10.3390/w17131980).
- **Chile 2016:** only delayed seeding of juveniles and towing cages to sanctuary sites were effective [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-19461-4).
- **Physical barriers:**
    - Skirts, upwelling and bubble curtains worked at some sites in Canada but failed on Chilean and Irish sites [21](https://doi.org/10.3390/w17131980).
    - Deep cages with semi-permeable skirts (55 m deep, skirt to 10 m) have been used with variable results [20](https://www.globalseafood.org/advocate/ca-technology-protect-salmon-farms-from-harmful-algal-blooms/).
- **Clay flocculation** is a physical method to contain active blooms [21](https://doi.org/10.3390/w17131980).
- **Site selection and forecasting:** risk assessment for Scottish farms identified Shetland as the highest combined HAB risk area [17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11768604/).

### Case Studies

- **Chile 2016, *Pseudochattonella* cf. *verruculosa*:**
    - Worst farmed fish-killing HAB on record, in Los Lagos (Reloncaví Sound).
    - About 39,942 t of salmon lost, roughly 12% of Chilean production, with losses above US$800 million [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-19461-4) [2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0048969720379146) [3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10458688/).
- **Chile 2021, *H. akashiwo*, Comau fjord:**
    - Ten farms affected, about 6,000 t of salmon lost, with 70,000 to 210,000 cells per mL [4](https://i-mar.cl/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/2023_Diaz_etal_STOTEN.pdf).
- **Norway 2019, *C. leadbeateri*:**
    - Most devastating fish-killing bloom recorded in Norway, in Nordland and Troms (May to June).
    - About 14,500 t of farmed salmon lost, with losses up to 2.4 billion NOK (about US$220 million).
    - A cold-water ribotype caused both the 1991 and 2019 blooms [9](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1568988322001159) [22](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1568988324001422).
- **Norway 1989, *P. parvum*, Sandsfjord:**
    - About 750 t of Atlantic salmon and rainbow trout died in a brackish fjord system, with repeated blooms in later summers [12](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10302719/).
- **Norway 2025:** first farm affected by the northern Norway bloom, with *Phaeocystis pouchetii* and *C. leadbeateri* in water samples [11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13331527/).
- **British Columbia and Puget Sound:** *H. akashiwo* blooms recur yearly since the 1960s, with US$2 to 6 million per event [19](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/71988/noaa_71988_DS1.pdf).
- **New Zealand 2010:** about 200 t of Chinook salmon died in Queen Charlotte Sound (*P. verruculosa*). Moving the pens to a bloom-free site saw surviving fish recover [13](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9147682/).
- **Scotland 2001:** *K. mikimotoi* killed about 500,000 salmon in Shetland [17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11768604/).
- **Galicia, Spain:** first *O. speculum* bloom recorded to cause salmon mortality [14](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10467100/).

## Data Insights

### Disease Impact by Country

#### Chile

- 2016 event: about 12% of national production lost [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-19461-4).
- Main ichthyotoxic species: *P. verruculosa*, *H. akashiwo*, *Karenia* spp. [3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10458688/).
- Also affecting farms: *Leptocylindrus minimus* and *Alexandrium catenella*, with lower mortality [3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10458688/).
- Fish kills in Patagonia were first reported after the salmon industry began in 1982 [6](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10892511/).
- Recent events: 2017 *Karenia* (Gulf of Penas) [26](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12654718/), 2021 *H. akashiwo* (Comau) [4](https://i-mar.cl/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/2023_Diaz_etal_STOTEN.pdf).

#### Norway

- Largest recorded event: 2019, northern Norway, about 14,500 t [9](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1568988322001159).
- Repeat events in 1991, 2019 and 2025 in the north [9](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1568988322001159) [11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13331527/).
- Brackish-water *P. parvum* blooms in Sandsfjord (south-west) since 1989 [12](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10302719/).

#### Other regions

- British Columbia, New Zealand, Scotland and Galicia: see Case Studies.

## Research and References

### Latest Research Findings

- **Nutrients and toxicity:** multi-omics of a *C. leadbeateri* bloom links nutrient depletion and mixotrophy to toxin production [10](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12190008/).
- **Toxin nature:** characterisation of the microalgal toxins from *C. leadbeateri* blooms in northern Norwegian fjords (preprint) [24](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.02.08.576530.full.pdf).
- **Recurrence and climate:** review of bloom recurrence in Chilean fjords [3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10458688/).
- **Remote sensing:** satellite and field data to detect *Karenia* blooms in Patagonia [26](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12654718/).
